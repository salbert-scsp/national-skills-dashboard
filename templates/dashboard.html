Overview

When people talk about Artificial Intelligence in the workplace, the conversation usually focuses on broad, dramatic predictions: Which jobs will disappear? Which job titles are safe? In reality, technology rarely eliminates entire occupations overnight. Instead, it reshapes the individual tasks and daily competencies that make up a job. A Software Engineer, an Accountant, and a Legal Assistant might keep their job titles for decades, but the specific software, tools, and workflows they use every day are changing rapidly. This phenomenon is called skill drift. Because job titles can be slow to update and labor market statistics often lag years behind real-world trends, evaluating workplace transformation by job title alone leads to blind spots. This dashboard cuts through surface-level job titles to measure the underlying technical DNA of everyday work. By mapping standard workplace skills against the modern AI ecosystem, we can track real-time workplace changes empirically—giving workers, educators, and policy planners the clear data they need to navigate the future of work.

How to Read

The macro view of the dashboard measures the fundamental AI proximity of every skill across the entire labor market, categorizing them into absolute technical tiers regardless of specific job titles. Color-coding serves as the primary truth here: Core AI Skills (Dark Blue) represent direct machine learning and natural language processing capabilities, AI-Enabling Skills (Dark Red) highlight the essential data infrastructure and cloud backend required to power intelligence, and Non-AI Skills (Grey) anchor traditional operational tools. High-scoring skills on this global spectrum point to areas where generative capabilities or automated processing are heavily concentrated, indicating high potential for workflow automation. Conversely, low-scoring skills represent legacy tools and human-centered tasks that remain largely unaffected by current AI developments. Looking at the macro landscape helps readers identify which competencies are driving broad workforce transformation and where proactive, AI-augmented upskilling offers the highest strategic value.

    Job Specific Skill Tracker

    When filtering the dashboard by a specific occupation, the visual bar gauges rescale from 0.00 to 1.00 to highlight which competencies dominate that particular role, but the underlying color coding remains fixed to preserve universal context. If a skill ranking near 1.00 in your role is colored Dark Red, it signifies that even the most dominant tool in your daily workflow operates in a low-AI exposure domain, meaning your role relies heavily on traditional, human-anchored capabilities. Alternatively, if your lowest-ranked skills are Medium Blue or Dark Blue, your field is deeply intertwined with the AI ecosystem, making AI integration essential across your entire work output. High scores within your specific job profile indicate tasks best approached by learning how to partner with AI assistants to boost execution speed, while lower scores highlight baseline domain knowledge that can be mastered without immediate concern for technological disruption.

      How we got the numbers

      This score shows how closely a skill connects to the world of artificial intelligence. Rather than showing raw similarity values, most panels scale the scores relative to each other so the most AI-intensive skills rise to the top.

      • Occupational skill tracking. The pipeline pulls the tools and technologies O*NET records against each occupation, so the data reflects catalogued workplace practice rather than a fixed list.
      • The AI proximity engine. Every skill's name and its vetted definition are read together, and the system measures the conceptual distance between that text and modern AI technologies.
      • Why the scores are normalized. Raw similarity values cluster tightly; even an unambiguously AI tool may only register around 0.47. Most panels therefore stretch the visible range onto 0.00 to 1.00.
      • Reading a 1.00. It does not mean a skill is entirely artificial intelligence. It means it is the highest AI-correlated skill within the current view.

      Normalization

      Bar length uses min-max normalization across the filtered slice:

      normalized = (x - min) / (max - min)

      Two consequences worth knowing. The lowest skill in any view renders as 0.00, which reflects its rank in that slice and not a zero score. And because the window is the filtered slice, a skill's bar changes as you filter while its raw score never does. Raw values are always shown in the tooltip, the detail panel, and every table view.

      Embedding and similarity

      Skill titles and definitions are encoded into 384-dimensional vectors by a local all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model running under ONNX Runtime, with mean pooling over the token embeddings so description length does not distort the result. Vectors are L2-normalized, which reduces cosine similarity to a dot product:

      similarity = A · B (for unit vectors)

      The AI score is a winner-take-all maximum against two poles, AI Engineering and Generative AI, measured across both the skill's name and its definition. Taking the maximum keeps a strong signal in either text from being diluted by the other.

      Further anchors describe how a skill is adjacent to AI rather than how close it is, and are reported alongside the score without feeding into it. Blending them into one number would change every score already recorded and make the history incomparable.

      They are named here for what they measure, which is not the same as what they are named after. Language and tooling asks whether a definition reads like a programming language or numerical library. ML infrastructure asks whether it reads like orchestration and deployment prose -- it ranks Kubernetes and MapReduce highest, and it reads low for ChatGPT, because ChatGPT's article describes a chatbot product and never mentions compute clusters. That is the correct reading of that text, not a fault. Both live in a range of roughly −0.09 to +0.48, which is why the drawer prints the range beside each number: a bare four-decimal figure invites being read as a percentage.

      A fourth anchor, conventional document software, is measured and recorded but reads on nothing. It exists because the generative pole repeatedly mistook word processors and transcription tools for AI, and it separates the two cleanly; it is being collected for a quarter before anyone argues it should decide anything.

      Scores are stored signed, so genuine anti-correlation is preserved rather than clipped to zero.

      Whether a tool embeds AI is searched for, not scored

      The AI embedded? column is the one input on this dashboard that is not a measurement. It comes from a web search for the product followed by a reading of the results, and it is the only thing that decides the Non-Technical Embedded AI class. A confirmed finding also adds a flat 0.05 to the raw AI score, shown broken out in the detail drawer.

      It used to be scored, and that is why it changed. The Embedded AI similarity column measures how a definition reads against vocabulary like workspace, workflow, integration and automated, which is the vocabulary of almost any productivity or design tool. A third of every skill in the store cleared the old threshold, and the class it produced contained LaTeX, EndNote and a 2D drafting package. Most of these definitions were written before the features they were being judged on existed. The similarity is still shown, beside the tag, because the disagreement between them is informative.

      Not checked is a real answer and not a blank. A product nobody has searched for and a product searched and found to ship no AI features are different facts, and only one of them says anything about the product. Searches get rate limited, so an unchecked skill is treated exactly as a negative for classification and never counted as one.

      Interpreting the three classes

      • AI Skill. Cognitive modelling, machine learning frameworks, transformer architectures.
      • AI Enabling. The infrastructure, MLOps, and data platforms required to deploy and scale that intelligence.
      • Not an AI skill. Legacy workflows and domain tools that remain anchored to human judgement.
      A calibration caveat, corrected against measurement. This previously said arbitrary English scores 0.10 to 0.15 against the AI poles as a floor effect. It does not: neutral English measures about 0.00, and there is no uniform floor. What is elevated is text about language specifically. Non-language software of ordinary specificity sits at 0.10 to 0.14, which is the band to treat as noise.

      Human review

      A skill reaches these panels only after its reference page clears a cross-encoder relevance gate and its source passes a credibility audit, or after a human approves it in the review queue. Nothing is scored before that point.
